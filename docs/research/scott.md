# Scott source notes

James C. Scott's *Against the Grain: A Deep History of the Earliest States*
examines the ecological and political arrangements behind early agrarian states.
Its main contribution to this game is a challenge: more concentrated production,
more people and stronger administration need not mean a better life for everyone.

## SCT-01 Settlement is not a fixed sequence

**Source:** introduction, paragraph 10; chapter 1, “Wetlands and Sedentism,”
paragraph 23, and “Minding the Gap,” paragraphs 42 and 50; chapter 2, paragraphs
3 and 8. Files `text/part0010.html`, `text/part0011.html`, `text/part0012.html`.

Scott separates domestication, settled communities, cultivation and states.
They did not appear together as one package. He emphasizes early wetland
settlements drawing on diverse resources, rather than treating centralized
irrigation of dry land as the necessary starting point.

**Game interpretation:** the opening need not be a hungry, ignorant group
waiting to discover farming. Food collection, planting, fishing and trade can
coexist. A linear tutorial can teach the interface without claiming a universal
historical sequence. The wetland case is an example, not a mandatory world map.

## SCT-02 Fire and dependencies

**Source:** chapter 1, “Fire,” paragraphs 4 and 14; chapter 2, paragraphs 8 and
18. Files `text/part0011.html`, `text/part0012.html`.

Fire changed habitat use, food preparation and subsequent crafts. Cultivation
and domestication created dependencies: selected plants and animals required
continued protection and care, while people increasingly depended on them.

**Game interpretation:** early techniques can already be present. Later
upgrades can improve their reliability and scale. More capable infrastructure
can create new support work; it need not be a benefit with no continuing inputs.

## SCT-03 Mixed subsistence and knowledge

**Source:** chapter 1, paragraphs 35, 40 and 50; chapter 2, paragraphs 8 and
43-56. Files `text/part0011.html`, `text/part0012.html`.

Scott describes resource diversity, exchange and skilled environmental
management among communities outside centralized agrarian states. He argues
that narrower subsistence routines could also narrow people's practical
engagement with their surroundings. His broader biological analogies in
chapter 2 are explicitly speculative.

**Game interpretation:** gathering, cultivation and social learning can coexist.
Make Education and Culture nodes represent an expansion of deliberate
investment, access or institutions. Their unlock should not imply that previous
generations lacked knowledge or culture. The particular bonuses remain game
design choices.

## SCT-04 Population and welfare

**Source:** chapter 3, “Drudgery and Its History,” paragraphs 4 and 7, and
“A Note on Fertility and Population,” paragraphs 39-42.
File `text/part0013.html`.

Scott argues that sedentary farming populations could grow despite arduous
work and disease burdens, with fertility contributing to their demographic
advantage over long periods. Demographic success is not a direct measure of
health, freedom or leisure. He distinguishes evidence from possible
explanations for the expansion of farming communities.

**Game interpretation:** Population is a useful count and progression resource,
but should not silently stand for universal well-being. Food and housing
thresholds are a gameplay model of support, not a historical law of fertility.
Avoid deriving reproduction rates from these qualitative arguments.

## SCT-05 Grain and legibility

**Source:** chapter 4, “Grains Make States,” paragraphs 29-38.
File `text/part0014.html`.

Scott argues that grains' visibility, storage, transport, divisibility and
harvest timing made them unusually convenient for assessment and appropriation.
The argument concerns what rulers could collect, as well as what cultivators
could grow. He distinguishes a population concentration from a state.

**Game interpretation:** a storable surplus can enable record keeping,
distribution and specialist support. Grain stores can also concentrate losses
and political power. A generic Food meter may abstract these differences;
do not claim that it proves all states required one identical crop system.

## SCT-06 Administration and power

**Source:** chapter 4, paragraphs 21, 47-48 and 52; chapter 5, paragraphs 17,
19, 24 and 46. Files `text/part0014.html`, `text/part0015.html`.

Surplus could support people outside direct food production. Writing and
measurement also enabled taxation and labor control, and walls could confine
as well as protect. Scott emphasizes coercion and scarce labor, while
acknowledging disputes about the extent and forms of bondage in different
places and periods.

**Game interpretation:** Organization can represent coordination in this game
without being equated with every historical state. Describe automatic building
as scheduling or shared practice. If later political choices appear, assess who
gets the benefit and bears the work. These notes do not prescribe adding war,
slavery, coercion or any other new system to the current scope.

## SCT-07 Concentration and fragility

**Source:** chapter 3, paragraphs 10-15 and 36; chapter 6, “Ecocide:
Deforestation and Salinization,” paragraphs 23-34.
Files `text/part0013.html`, `text/part0016.html`.

Dense settlements and simplified food webs could amplify shared risks. Scott
discusses disease, crop vulnerability, fuel demand, erosion, siltation and
salinization. The evidence for particular prehistoric epidemics and causes of
collapse is often indirect; these are not secure diagnoses of every abandoned
site.

**Game interpretation:** diversity, reserves, maintenance and access to several
resources can matter alongside maximum production. If stress is added later,
show its cause and a recovery action. A universal random collapse timer or a
constant salinity penalty would not follow from the regional evidence.

## SCT-08 Collapse and survival

**Source:** chapter 6, paragraphs 3, 12, 34 and 50 under “Praising Collapse.”
File `text/part0016.html`.

Scott separates the breakdown of a state from the death or disappearance of
all its people. Dispersal and smaller political units could survive an
institutional collapse, and in some cases could offer an escape from burdens.
This is not an argument that all collapses were benign.

**Game interpretation:** if prestige represents reorganization, preserving
people and some capabilities is more plausible than treating every reset as
total extinction. The repeatable exchange of supplies for permanent bonuses
remains a game invention. Use this passage as an analogy for continuity,
not historical proof of prestige mechanics.

## SCT-09 Trade and nonstate neighbors

**Source:** chapter 1, paragraph 35; chapter 7, paragraphs 12, 24, 54, 56,
65 and 82. Files `text/part0011.html`, `text/part0017.html`.

Scott treats “barbarian” as a label used by states, not a lower cultural stage.
Mobile and nonstate peoples traded, raided, negotiated and supplied services;
relations with states included both dependence and exploitation. Early
settlements were not necessarily self-sufficient or isolated.

**Game interpretation:** exchange does not have to wait for modern money, and
neighboring communities need not exist merely as obstacles or populations to
absorb. A later map could support mutually useful trade and different strategies.
Use neutral descriptions of communities rather than reproducing a hierarchy
of “civilized” and “primitive.”
