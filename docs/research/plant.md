# Plant source notes

John Plant's *Primitive Technology: A Survivalist's Guide to Building Tools,
Shelters, and More in the Wild* supplies concrete examples of materials,
equipment and work sequences. Use it to give Craft and hut-building projects
recognizable dependencies. The projects are modern reconstructions and personal
experiments, with many materials drawn from Australia; they are not a universal
prehistoric technology tree or measured game production rates.

## PLT-01 Local materials and reconstruction

**Source:** introduction, paragraphs 2-5, `OEBPS/xhtml/intro.xhtml`.

Plant distinguishes techniques adapted from earlier practice from his own
outdoor experiments. He notes Australian materials, possible substitutions and
constraints that shaped his designs.

**Game use:** choose a material palette for a scenario. Saplings, bark, grass,
cane, clay and building stone can be alternatives with different work or upkeep
implications. Do not turn the order of projects in this book into historical dates.

## PLT-02 Tools enable other work

**Source:** part 1, “Hammer Stone,” paragraphs 7-13, and “Digging Stick,”
paragraphs 61-64, `OEBPS/xhtml/c01.xhtml`; part 5, “Adze,” paragraphs 6-16,
`OEBPS/xhtml/c05.xhtml`.

The examples connect tools to jobs: shaping stone, digging soil or clay, and
working wood. The adze itself needs suitable stone, a handle and lashing.

**Game use:** a named Craft improvement can expand access or reduce work on
particular projects. Basic tools can already be available at the start. Neither
a new metal tier nor a generic bonus to every resource is required for each
improvement. Preserve the distinction between making a tool and using it.

## PLT-03 Basketry and cordage connect several branches

**Source:** part 4, paragraphs 9-13, 22-27, 38-44 and 52-63,
`OEBPS/xhtml/c04.xhtml`.

Basket types use different materials and weaving methods. Plant describes uses
for carrying bulky goods and containing smaller seeds, and connects twining to
fish traps. Cordage has uses in lashings, bows and drills.

**Game use:** basket-making knowledge can support carrying, storage and trap
construction without making them the same purchase. A loosely woven carrying
basket is not automatically a grain store or watertight vessel. Sharing a craft
prerequisite does not make the costs and effects identical.

## PLT-04 A hut is a bundle of tasks

**Source:** part 6, “Dome Hut,” paragraphs 91-92 and its illustration;
“Gabled Roof Hut,” paragraphs 135-136, `OEBPS/xhtml/c06.xhtml`.

The dome example combines pliable saplings, anchoring, lashings and thatch.
The gabled example uses a different frame and separates sleeping, storage and
work uses. These are particular small shelters, not a standard number of people
housed by every historical hut.

**Game use:** the current labor-only hut can summarize gathering materials,
preparing members, erecting a frame and covering it. Future projects could
separate frame, roof or storage improvements. The game's four housing places,
one builder and build-time coefficients are authored abstractions.

## PLT-05 Walls depend on weather protection

**Source:** part 6, “Mud Wall,” paragraph 34; “Wattle & Daub Wall,” paragraph
51; weather-protection notes, paragraphs 69-70, `OEBPS/xhtml/c06.xhtml`.

Plant describes mud walls as vulnerable to rain and explains why a roof may
need to come first. His wattle-and-daub example combines a woven frame and mud,
with different material, speed and strength characteristics from a solid wall.

**Game use:** wall and roof choices can interact. A lighter wall need not simply
be a strictly better tier. Weather resistance or repair work would be a possible
future dimension, not an effect currently implemented by buying a hut.

## PLT-06 Roofing has its own materials and processes

**Source:** part 6, “How to Make Flat Ceramic Roof Tiles,” paragraph 171;
“How to Make Barrel Roof Tiles,” paragraph 179, `OEBPS/xhtml/c06.xhtml`.

Plant explains his switch from perishable thatch to fired-clay roofing and then
his reasons for using a different tile form. Tiles depend on clay preparation,
firing and a frame that supports them.

**Game use:** a roof upgrade can exchange more initial work and firing inputs
for a different durability profile. Avoid making every roof material a universal
linear improvement or giving it a historical date from this modern experiment.

## PLT-07 Traps require fabrication and visits

**Source:** part 3, “Fish Trap,” paragraphs 40-53, `OEBPS/xhtml/c03.xhtml`;
part 4, “Twining Weave Basket,” paragraph 41, `OEBPS/xhtml/c04.xhtml`.

The trap is a woven container with a funnel. Plant distinguishes making it
from placing, checking, collecting from and reusing it. The description is not
a measured catch-rate experiment.

**Game use:** it gives the current river-trap project a credible craft basis.
Fabrication cost, equipment capacity and collection labor are different design
variables. The current staffed Fish route and deterministic doubled catch are
game choices; this source does not justify worker-free or guaranteed food.

## PLT-08 Hunting equipment is not a food-return measurement

**Source:** part 3, “Spear Thrower,” paragraphs 54-55,
`OEBPS/xhtml/c03.xhtml`; introduction, paragraph 5.

Plant explains the mechanical purpose of a spear thrower and describes legal
restrictions that limited his use of animal products. His tool demonstrations
do not establish a community's actual hunting success or subsistence returns.

**Game use:** equipment, skill, search and crew coordination can be separate
contributors to Hunt. Use Kelly for return-rate accounting and contextual
variation; choose the game's crew thresholds and rewards through balance work.

## PLT-09 Cooking can precede pottery

**Source:** part 2, “Burn Bowl,” paragraphs 99-107 and “How to Boil Water Using
a Burn Bowl,” paragraphs 117-128, `OEBPS/xhtml/c02.xhtml`.

Plant presents a wooden container and a stone-boiling example that does not
require a ceramic cooking vessel. This supplies a process example, not evidence
that all societies adopted it or a general drinking-water treatment protocol.

**Game use:** fire, containers, preparation and pottery need not unlock as one
package. An early Food balance can stand for edible provisions after preparation;
a future cooking project can improve a specific process rather than invent cooking.
See also [KEL-03](kelly.md#kel-03-processing-and-attention).

## PLT-10 Firing needs a supporting process

**Source:** part 7, “Reusable Charcoal Mound,” paragraphs 7-11; “Updraft Kiln,”
paragraphs 78-82; “How to Use a Kiln” and “How to Make Ceramics,” paragraphs
100-103, `OEBPS/xhtml/c07.xhtml`.

The examples link fuel preparation, drying, heating and cooling to usable
ceramics. Plant describes a kiln as providing a more controlled firing
environment than a simple pit. The particular materials and firing results
depend on the setup; they do not define a universal ceramic progression.

**Game use:** Craft can abstract the work embodied in fired-clay jars. More
detailed later pottery could distinguish preparation, fuel and firing capacity.
Do not derive a game work rate or automatic stoneware/glaze unlock from a
single modern recipe. The existing [water-storage material distinction](reference-guide.md#water-storage-materials)
still applies.
