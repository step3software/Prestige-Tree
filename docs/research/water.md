# Water collection, storage and heat

Water progression can reward three different investments: equipment made with
Craft, shared works coordinated through Organization, and heat supplied by Fire.
Keep access, transport, storage and treatment separate so each investment solves
a recognizable problem. A larger jar holds more water; a lifting device reduces
drawing work; a downhill channel can replace carrying.

These are research-backed design options. The [current Water rules](../civilization.md#water-hauling-jars-and-flow)
remain authoritative; prices, unlocks and effects below are proposals, not changes
to the game. The examples span different places and periods rather than one
universal invention sequence.

## What each cost can mean

| Cost | Proposed meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Craft | Fabrication, fitting and repair of equipment or linings. | Water baskets, jars, bucket ropes, lever lifts, pipe sections. |
| Organization | Coordinating shared construction, access, maintenance and distribution. | Communal cisterns, catchment cleaning, drawing schedules, channel maintenance. |
| Fire | Access to controlled heat, or expenditure of fuel and heating work. | Firing jars or pipes during manufacture; repeatedly heating water during treatment. |
| Work | Time spent constructing or operating the system. | Digging, weaving, carrying, drawing, cleaning and tending heat. |

Organization need not imply a state or the invention of cooperation. A proposed
water rota can improve timely deliveries without lowering anyone's biological
water requirement. Craft and Organization can be spent together when a project
needs both equipment and shared work.

Fire is not an active resource in the inspected opening. The clearest initial
design would be a **controlled-fire capability** required for fired vessels, with
fuel gathering and firing work included in construction. If Fire becomes a
spendable resource, define it as fuel prepared for useful heating and distinguish
one-time firing costs from recurring treatment costs. The historical sources do
not set an exchange rate between Fire, Craft and Organization.

Source links explain the underlying technique, not a calibrated multiplier.

| Technique | Suggested costs | Effect worth testing | Dependency or tradeoff |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tightly coiled water baskets | Craft + weaving work | More carried Water per trip; an alternative early container. | Only sufficiently tight construction holds water; ordinary food baskets do not automatically qualify. [WAT-01](#wat-01-containers-before-pottery) |
| Wooden burn bowls | Craft + Fire during fabrication | Small local reserves or a vessel for heating water. | Treat capacity, portability and treatment as separate choices. [WAT-01](#wat-01-containers-before-pottery), [WAT-07](#wat-07-heat-and-treatment) |
| Earthenware jars | Craft + Fire during manufacture | Larger reserve capacity. | A container must be filled; porous earthenware is not inherently watertight. [WAT-02](#wat-02-fired-clay-and-retention) |
| Improved ceramic bodies | More Craft + controlled firing | Better retention from a suitable less-permeable body. | Requires suitable clay and firing control; simply adding heat to any jar is not a universal upgrade. [WAT-02](#wat-02-fired-clay-and-retention) |
| Covered reserve vessels | Craft for covers + Organization for storage practice | Better reserve protection if contamination/exposure is later modeled. | Does not increase collection or establish drinking-water safety by itself. [WAT-06](#wat-06-storage-practice) |
| Shared lined cistern | Organization + Craft + substantial construction work | A larger buffer for interruptions. | Stores delivered or harvested water; does not tap groundwater merely by being underground. [WAT-05](#wat-05-cisterns-channels-and-pipes) |
| Rain catchment and gutters | Craft + Organization | Rain events refill available storage with less carrying. | Needs rainfall, a suitable catchment surface and a receiving store. Keep roof area and tank capacity separate. [WAT-04](#wat-04-rain-catchment-and-seasonality) |
| Timber-lined well | Craft + Organization + digging work | Access to an appropriate groundwater source. | Lining supports the excavation; water still has to be raised. The cited example does not require metal woodworking tools. [WAT-03](#wat-03-wells-are-access-works) |
| Rope bucket and support | Craft + construction work | Faster or easier drawing from an existing source. | Still needs an operator and carrying to the settlement. [WAT-08](#wat-08-lifting-water) |
| Counterweighted lever lift | Craft; Organization for a larger installation | Less drawing work per delivery, or a faster cycle. | Human-powered lifting is not unattended production. [WAT-08](#wat-08-lifting-water) |
| Spring channel | Organization + Craft + construction work | Deliver water by gravity and release hauling labor. | Needs an available source above the destination and a maintained route; flow remains finite. [WAT-05](#wat-05-cisterns-channels-and-pipes) |
| Fired-clay distribution pipes | Craft + Fire during manufacture + Organization | Extend a suitable supply to users or storage. | Pipes convey water; they do not supply the source or lift it without energy. [WAT-05](#wat-05-cisterns-channels-and-pipes) |
| Water rota and reserve keeper | Organization + ongoing assigned work | More reliable dispatches and access during competing demands. | Scheduling does not itself eliminate hauling work; this is a design interpretation of coordination. |
| Stone boiling or direct vessel heating | Fire/fuel + a suitable vessel + attended work | Treatment if a future water-quality mechanic needs it. | Recurring heat cost; treatment does not add stored volume. Pottery need not gate all water heating. [WAT-07](#wat-07-heat-and-treatment) |

For the present opening, prioritize **container choices, a drawing improvement
and a shared storage/catchment choice**. They can create different investments
without requiring a separate quality simulation. Preserve the distinction
between storage and income in their descriptions. Gravity delivery can remain a
larger Organization/Craft project whose site requirements are abstracted.

## Relationship to current Civ

At the inspected source revision, carrying yokes already reduce the Water crew;
earthenware jars add capacity; wells improve trips; a keeper repeats collection;
and the Gravity-fed spring replaces hauling. The Emergency cistern changes
Water-shortage grace rather than adding a separately filled reserve. Calling a
new capacity project a cistern must not silently change that existing upgrade's
meaning. [Current design](../civilization.md#water-hauling-jars-and-flow),
[shortage rules](../civilization.md#shortages-and-recovery).

A useful balance comparison is the same as for Food expeditions:
`Water delivered / (crew × full cycle time)`. Compare that with construction work,
Craft/Organization expenditure and usable reserve duration. A reserve's duration
depends on **current stored Water and demand**, not empty capacity. A proposed
catchment also needs a rainfall assumption; a proposed lift still needs a water
source. Numerical prices and unlock order belong in a subsequent design change
with the [content balance workflow](../balance-testing.md#add-balance-coverage-with-content).

## Passage and source notes

Book locators use the existing [source reader](README.md#open-a-cited-passage).
Web sources were consulted on 2026-11-27. These notes separate examples of
historical use, material properties and modern treatment knowledge.

### WAT-01 Containers before pottery

Plant's coil-weave basket can hold water only when sufficiently tightly woven;
he describes it as more time-consuming than ordinary basket weaving. His burn
bowl uses fire to hollow wood. These support different container recipes rather
than making all water transport depend on ceramic jars. Plant is describing
modern practical reconstructions, not dating the first use of either technique.

Passages: Plant part 4, `OEBPS/xhtml/c04.xhtml`, paragraphs 22–39; part 2,
`OEBPS/xhtml/c02.xhtml`, paragraphs 99–107. Related [PLT-03](plant.md#plt-03-basketry-and-cordage-connect-several-branches)
and [PLT-09](plant.md#plt-09-cooking-can-precede-pottery).

### WAT-02 Fired clay and retention

The [Ashmolean ceramic classification](https://potweb.ashmolean.org/PotABCs1.html)
describes porous earthenware and a fused, waterproof stoneware body, while noting
that only certain clays tolerate higher firing. The [Met's storage jar](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/448695)
illustrates unglazed earthenware storing and cooling liquid through evaporation
at its porous walls. That object is from 8th–10th-century Iraq, not evidence for
the first water jar. Retention and evaporative cooling can therefore be different
design objectives; cooling consumes water rather than creating it.

Plant's updraft kiln separates the firebox and ware chamber and improves firing
control over his pit-firing example: part 7, `OEBPS/xhtml/c07.xhtml`, paragraphs
78–82. This supports a kiln as a fabrication investment; it does not establish
that every kiln produces stoneware. See [PLT-10](plant.md#plt-10-firing-needs-a-supporting-process).

### WAT-03 Wells are access works

[Tegel and colleagues' 2012 excavation study](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0051374)
documents four early Neolithic wooden wells in eastern Germany. Its results
describe hollow-trunk and joined-timber linings, with stone-adze woodworking and
complex corner joints. This is evidence for sophisticated well construction
without metal tools. It supports Craft-intensive access works; an Organization
cost is a proposed abstraction of shared construction, not a demonstrated
political requirement.

### WAT-04 Rain catchment and seasonality

FAO's [rural water-supply engineering chapter](https://www.fao.org/4/S1250E/S1250E1d.htm),
under Catchment Areas and Storage Requirements, relates collection to rainfall,
catchment area and surface properties. Storage bridges intermittent supply, and
dry years may leave tanks partly empty. These principles support event-driven
refill and a tradeoff between catchment size and storage size. Its modern cement
and steel tank recipes are not early craft technologies. Its older drinking-water
safety statements are not used here; current CDC guidance below supplies the
quality distinction.

### WAT-05 Cisterns channels and pipes

AMNH's [Petra water-system account](https://www.amnh.org/explore/ology/archaeology/a-walk-through-the-ruins-of-petra),
under Water Pipe and The Siq, describes terracotta pipes, rock-cut channels and
elevated cisterns/reservoirs distributing water by gravity. Its [city overview](https://www.amnh.org/explore/ology/archaeology/the-ancient-city-of-petra2)
also describes underground stores that limit evaporation. Petra is a later urban
example: it demonstrates how collection, storage and conveyance fit together,
not an opening settlement's construction scale or mandatory progression.

### WAT-06 Storage practice

The CDC's [rainwater overview](https://www.cdc.gov/drinking-water/about/collecting-rainwater-and-your-health-an-overview.html)
distinguishes the quantity collected from its suitability for use: collection
surfaces and storage can introduce germs or chemicals. Cleaning, repair and
screening are maintenance activities, not proof that collected rainwater is safe
to drink. This supports a possible Organization investment in upkeep without
turning a roof catchment into automatically potable supply.

### WAT-07 Heat and treatment

Plant heats water with hot stones in a wooden burn bowl: part 2,
`OEBPS/xhtml/c02.xhtml`, paragraphs 117–128. Smil also discusses hot stones in
water-filled leather pouches: chapter 2, `OPS/c10752_002.xhtml`, paragraph 17.
These examples separate water heating from a ceramic-container prerequisite.
Kelly's processing discussion distinguishes overlapping elapsed time from work
requiring attention: chapter 3, printed page 64, physical PDF page 86.

For treatment effects, the CDC's [emergency water guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/water-emergency/about/)
states that boiling kills germs but does not make water with fuel, toxic chemicals
or radioactive contamination safe. Settling or cloth straining should not become
a universal purification upgrade. A game abstraction can model heat as recurring
fuel and work without asserting that all heated or visibly clear water is safe.

### WAT-08 Lifting water

Smil chapter 3, `OPS/c10752_003.xhtml`, paragraphs 98–102, contrasts gravity
irrigation with the labor of raising water, then describes scoops, buckets and
the counterweighted wooden lever known as a shaduf. These examples concern
irrigation; applying their lifting principle to the game's general Water supply
is a design inference. A lever can reduce drawing effort without eliminating an
operator, the source constraint or transport from the lifting point. Animal- and
flow-powered devices offer later branches, but their energy sources and support
work need explicit treatment rather than a generic free-water bonus.
